  <big><strong>Components of the Lorentz Group</big></strong> <br>

  The discussion began with the disconnected components of the Lorentz group. Lorentz transformations can be classified according to the sign of their determinant and whether they preserve the direction of time. These conditions produce four components associated with the identity-connected sector, parity, time reversal, and the combined parity–time-reversal transformation. <br>

  Continuous infinitesimal transformations are normally restricted to the proper orthochronous Lorentz group, which has determinant +1, preserves time orientation, and is continuously connected to the identity. Parity and time reversal are discrete transformations belonging to separate components and must therefore be treated independently. The transformation properties of delta functions and invariant integration measures under these transformations were also considered. <br><br>

  <big><strong>Noether Currents for Lorentz Transformations</big></strong> <br>

  Infinitesimal Lorentz transformations were used to derive the corresponding conserved Noether current. The variations of spacetime coordinates and fields were inserted into the variation of the Lagrangian, and the resulting terms were reorganized into a total divergence. <br>

  The antisymmetry of the infinitesimal Lorentz parameter played an important role in the derivation. In particular, the contraction of an antisymmetric Lorentz parameter with a symmetric tensor vanishes, simplifying the variation and identifying the conserved current associated with Lorentz invariance. <br><br>

  <big><strong>Angular Momentum and the Energy–Momentum Tensor</big></strong> <br>

  The Lorentz current was decomposed into orbital and intrinsic-spin contributions. Its orbital part was constructed from spacetime coordinates and the energy–momentum tensor, while the spin current accounted for the transformation of fields carrying nonzero spin. <br>

  The conserved charges associated with spatial rotations and Lorentz boosts were examined. This led to a discussion of the relationship between the relativistic center of energy, the center-of-momentum frame, and the boost generators. Antisymmetrization of the Lorentz indices was used to verify the structure of the angular-momentum current. <br><br>

  <big><strong>Total Derivatives and Improvement Terms</big></strong> <br>

  The effect of adding a total derivative to the Lagrangian was analyzed. Such an addition does not change the Euler–Lagrange equations under the usual boundary conditions, but it can modify the canonical energy–momentum tensor and the associated expression for the Noether current. <br>

  This observation motivated a discussion of improvement terms. A divergence term can be added to the canonical energy–momentum tensor without changing its conserved four-momentum, provided that the added superpotential has the appropriate antisymmetry. The resulting freedom shows that the local expression for the energy–momentum tensor is not generally unique. <br><br>

  <big><strong>Symmetrization of the Energy–Momentum Tensor</big></strong> <br>

  The canonical energy–momentum tensor was noted to be generally asymmetric. The problem of constructing an appropriate improvement term that makes it symmetric was therefore considered, including the Belinfante–Rosenfeld procedure, which combines the canonical tensor with derivatives of the spin current. <br>

  Questions were also raised about how gauge conditions, including the Lorenz gauge, affect the form of the improved tensor. The symmetry or antisymmetry required of the improvement superpotential was distinguished from the desired symmetry of the final energy–momentum tensor. The extent to which the improved tensor is unique, and how total-derivative modifications of the Lagrangian affect it, was left for further examination. <br><br>

  <big><strong>Scalar Potentials and Spontaneous Symmetry Breaking</big></strong> <br>

  A separate calculation involving a scalar-field Lagrangian and its potential was discussed. The ground state was determined by minimizing the potential, and a degenerate set of nonzero minima was interpreted in terms of spontaneous symmetry breaking, as in the standard Mexican-hat potential. <br>

  Several mathematical techniques for simplifying the relevant equations and integrals were reviewed, including integration by parts and trigonometric substitutions. Possible applications of similar potential-based descriptions to models of nuclear interactions were also considered, and the session concluded with a review of the remaining algebraic details. <br>
